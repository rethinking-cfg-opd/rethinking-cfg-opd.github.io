Rethinking Classifier-Free Guidance in On-Policy Diffusion Distillation

Abstract

On-policy distillation (OPD) has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for adapting diffusion models by querying a teacher along trajectories generated by the current student. However, its formulation under classifier-free guidance (CFG), a fundamental component of modern diffusion systems, remains poorly understood.

Existing OPD methods naturally extend velocity matching to the CFG-composed prediction, matching teacher and student guided velocities directly. We show that this objective is valid in standard text-to-image settings, where teacher and student remain aligned on the negative branch, but can fail in more general settings with asymmetric teacher–student conditioning. In such cases, CFG composition couples positive- and negative-branch discrepancies, allowing branch-level errors to cancel while producing identical guided supervision signals. We term this phenomenon Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA). To address it, we introduce Positive–Direction Matching (PDM), a branch-aware OPD objective that separately constrains the positive prediction and the CFG conditional direction. Experiments on FLUX-based visual knowledge distillation and dense-to-sparse video control demonstrate that naive guided matching suffers from CFG-scale sensitivity under NBA, while branch-aware objectives enable more robust and effective knowledge transfer.

Naive CFG-based OPD silently relies on teacher and student being aligned on the negative branch. We make this condition explicit — and show exactly when it holds and when it breaks.

When conditioning, capacity, or architecture make the negative branches differ, CFG composition lets positive- and negative-branch errors cancel, creating infinitely many degenerate solutions. We formalize this as NBA.

We introduce Positive–Direction Matching (PDM), which supervises the positive prediction and the CFG direction separately, plus an IBM baseline — both removing the cancellation ambiguity.

Background

On-policy distillation meets CFG

In diffusion on-policy distillation (OPD), the student first rolls out its own denoising trajectory; at every state it visits, a teacher supplies a local velocity target and the student is updated to match it.

But modern diffusion models run with classifier-free guidance (CFG): the velocity actually used at inference is a mix of a positive (conditional) and a negative (unconditional) branch.

\[ v_{\mathrm{cfg}} = \gamma\, v^{+} + (1-\gamma)\, v^{-} \]
CFG composition

Existing OPD methods simply match this composed velocity. Writing the per-branch teacher–student errors as \(e^{+} = v_T^{+} - v_S^{+}\) and \(e^{-} = v_T^{-} - v_S^{-}\), the naive per-state loss is a single squared term:

\[ \ell_{\mathrm{naive}} = \bigl\lVert\, \gamma\, e^{+} + (1-\gamma)\, e^{-} \,\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2} \]
Eq. 5
The full training objective averages this per-state loss \(\ell\) over the student-visited states and timesteps.

Why it usually works

In standard text-to-image distillation, teacher and student come from the same base model and share the same null-text negative branch, so \(e^{-}\!\approx 0\). The objective then reduces to plain positive-branch matching — and the method behaves well.

The problem

Negative Branch Asymmetry

In general OPD, teacher and student can differ in architecture, capacity, or conditioning — e.g. a teacher with privileged dense control distilled into a student that sees only sparse input. Then \(e^{-}\) is no longer negligible.

With a non-trivial negative branch, driving the naive loss to zero no longer forces the branches to match. It only requires a single linear relation:

\[ e^{+} = \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\, e^{-} \]
Eq. 6
— an entire line of non-zero \((e^{+}, e^{-})\) pairs, i.e. infinitely many degenerate solutions.

Loss of branch-level identifiability

Supervising only the CFG-composed direction cannot pin down the positive-branch error: a negative-branch error can be traded off against a positive-branch error without changing the loss value. We call this Negative Branch Asymmetry (NBA). Because the student only fits one particular guidance composition, it breaks when the inference guidance scale changes.

Our method

Positive–Direction Matching

Instead of supervising the merged velocity, PDM supervises two quantities that together determine the guided prediction at any scale: the positive prediction \(v^{+}\) and the CFG conditional direction \(d_M = v_M^{+} - v_M^{-}\).

\[ \ell_{\mathrm{PDM}} = \lVert e^{+}\rVert_{2}^{2} + \lambda\,\lVert d_T - d_S\rVert_{2}^{2} = \lVert e^{+}\rVert_{2}^{2} + \lambda\,\lVert e^{+} - e^{-}\rVert_{2}^{2} \]
Eq. 10

The first term anchors the positive prediction; the second preserves the conditional direction. For any \(\lambda > 0\), zero loss now requires \(e^{+} = e^{-} = 0\) — the cancellation ambiguity is eliminated and the supervision becomes identifiable.

A direct baseline — Independent Branch Matching (IBM)

IBM matches the two branches separately, \(\ell_{\mathrm{IBM}} = \gamma^{2}\lVert e^{+}\rVert_{2}^{2} + (\gamma-1)^{2}\lVert e^{-}\rVert_{2}^{2}\). For the guided regime \(\gamma > 1\) it shares PDM's unique zero-loss solution; the two differ only in how they weight the supervision.

Efficient partial-trajectory supervision

Rather than querying the teacher at every denoising step, we supervise only the first K student-visited states. A moderate horizon (K = 8) preserves distillation quality while cutting training cost by roughly 6× versus full-trajectory supervision.

Two asymmetric settings

Where NBA appears

We probe NBA in two settings where the teacher's privileged condition changes the negative branch as well as the positive one — placing them squarely outside the benign text-to-image regime.

Visual knowledge distillation

FLUX · reference-conditioned teacher → text-only student

The teacher sees a reference image r carrying a rendering style or a concept's identity; the student sees only the text prompt. The reference stays available even in the teacher's negative branch.

\[\begin{array}{ll} c_T^{+} = (y,\ \textcolor{#d1495b}{r}) & \hspace{2.2em} c_S^{+} = y \\[4pt] c_T^{-} = (\varnothing,\ \textcolor{#d1495b}{r}) & \hspace{2.2em} c_S^{-} = \varnothing \end{array}\]

Dense-to-sparse video control

Wan-VACE · dense-control teacher → 4-keyframe student

The teacher receives a dense per-frame control sequence; the student sees only 4 sparse keyframes. Dense vs. sparse control induces different negative predictions even under the same null text.

\[\begin{array}{ll} c_T^{+} = (y,\ \textcolor{#d1495b}{p_{\mathrm{dense}}}) & \hspace{2.2em} c_S^{+} = (y,\ p_{\mathrm{sparse}}) \\[4pt] c_T^{-} = (\varnothing,\ \textcolor{#d1495b}{p_{\mathrm{dense}}}) & \hspace{2.2em} c_S^{-} = (\varnothing,\ p_{\mathrm{sparse}}) \end{array}\]

Ablations

What matters in PDM

Two design choices, on the pose-only setting: the conditional-direction weight λ, and how many student-visited steps K receive teacher supervision.

Conditional-direction weight λ

λ = 0 is positive-only matching. λ = 1 gives the best fidelity/quality balance (lowest keyframe MPJPE, highest PCK@0.1); larger λ trades control accuracy for distributional quality.

Conclusion

Takeaways & limitations

Naive CFG-based OPD can hide branch-level errors through cross-branch cancellation — the failure mode we call Negative Branch Asymmetry. Positive–Direction Matching supervises the positive prediction and the CFG direction separately, restoring identifiable supervision that stays stable across inference guidance scales.

Limitations. The analysis covers linear CFG and two model families; the visual-knowledge results are primarily qualitative; and PDM and IBM share the same branch-level zero-loss solution, so PDM's advantage over IBM is currently empirical rather than theoretical.
